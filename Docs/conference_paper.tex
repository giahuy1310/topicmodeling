\section*{Abstract}
Fine-grained emotion recognition from Vietnamese social-media text is a difficult natural language processing problem due to the language's low-resource status. This paper addresses a methodological gap on this problem: existing ensembles typically fuse base-model probabilities with fixed rules or linear stacking and do not condition on the raw sentence, while large language models are used mainly as offline generators or as static prompts. We propose a two-stage framework, evaluated on UIT-VSMEC, a seven-class Vietnamese social-media emotion corpus. In Stage~1, Gemini-2.5-Flash generates and paraphrases minority-class examples via 10-shot prompting. In Stage~2, five heterogeneous base classifiers (PhoBERT, CafeBERT, ViBERT, Logistic Regression, LinearSVC) produce probability distributions, and a QLoRA-fine-tuned meta-learning Gemma-2-9B-it consumes the raw sentence and those five distributions jointly. The full framework reaches 70.33\% $\pm$ 1.33 accuracy, 70.39\% $\pm$ 1.26 weighted F1, and 67.27\% $\pm$ 1.25 macro F1. On the same settings, Logistic Regression stacking reaches 67.48\% $\pm$ 0.50 weighted F1 and weighted voting reaches 67.91\% $\pm$ 1.20.

Vietnamese NLP, emotion recognition, data augmentation, large language models,
ensemble learning, social media
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

The rapid growth of Vietnamese social-media platforms has produced vast volumes of user-generated text carrying rich emotional signals. Mining
these signals through fine-grained Emotion Recognition (ER) yields value for market
research, opinion monitoring, and mental health screening. However, ER remains substantially
harder than coarse sentiment polarity classification. Sentiment analysis on Vietnamese
text, which is usually coarse-grained emotion (positive, negative, neutral), has
benefited from larger annotated corpora, mature pre-trained language models, and
relatively clear lexical polarity cues. Hybrid architectures such as PhoBERT combined
with CNN-LSTM reach over 93\% accuracy on the UIT-VSFC
\cite{nguyen2018vsfc,nguyen2018deeplearning,nguyen2025phobert}, and several deep-learning
baselines report F1 scores above 90\% on comparable polarity benchmarks. By contrast, fine-grained emotion recognition explores more aspects of the emotion, where labels are linguistically subtler and more context-dependent. On UIT-VSMEC, the best reported result from the original benchmark study reaches only 59.74\% weighted F1 using CNN with word2vec embeddings
\cite{ho2020emotion}. Subsequent monolingual BERT models have narrowed but not closed this divide, indicating that scaling pre-training alone is insufficient for reliable fine-grained Vietnamese emotion classification.

Two obstacles drive this gap. First, Vietnamese is a low-resource language \cite{duong2021review,mai2025neuesc}: annotated datasets are scarce. Second, UIT-VSMEC suffers from pronounced class imbalance: the dominant label \textit{Enjoyment} constitutes roughly 28\% of the training set, whereas minority emotions such as \textit{Surprise} ($\sim$4.5\%) and \textit{Anger}
($\sim$6.9\%) are severely underrepresented. Together, these constraints limit the robustness and generalization of individual classifiers.

There exists a methodological gap in which current ensemble methods typically combine class probabilities using fixed rules, such as voting, or through linear models like logistic-regression stacking. In contrast, large language models (LLMs) are predominantly employed as offline data generators or as static prompts. We address this gap with a two-stage framework. In Stage~1, Gemini-2.5-Flash \cite{comanici2025gemini}
performs targeted data augmentation for minority emotion classes using 10-shot prompting. In Stage~2, five heterogeneous base classifiers produce probability estimates; a Gemma-2-9B-it \cite{gemma_2024} act as the meta learning model is then fine-tuned with parameter-efficient QLoRA \cite{dettmers2023qlora} on the raw sentence and those distributions jointly.

The main contributions of this paper are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item An LLM-based augmentation protocol that generates and paraphrases Vietnamese
        social-media sentences for minority emotion classes with a controlled
        10-shot prompting strategy.
  \item A stacked ensemble in which a QLoRA-fine-tuned meta learning model Gemma consumes the raw sentence and five base-model probability distributions.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Vietnamese Emotion Recognition}

Ho et al.\cite{ho2020emotion} introduced UIT-VSMEC, a 6,927-sentence seven-class
corpus, establishing a CNN+word2vec baseline of 59.74\% weighted F1. Nguyen and
Nguyen \cite{nguyen2020exploiting} reached 64.40\% through multi-label regression with preprocessing and key-clause extraction. Pre-trained transformers further narrowed the gap: PhoBERT \cite{nguyen2020phobert} underpins hybrid CNN-LSTM architectures that achieve 93.24\% accuracy on the polarity task \cite{nguyen2025phobert}, but the fine-grained ER on informal social-media text remains harder. CafeBERT \cite{do2024vlue} (XLM-RoBERTa trained on Vietnamese Wikipedia and news) reports 66.12\% F1 on VSMEC; ViSoBERT \cite{nguyen2024visobert} reaches 68.37\% weighted F1; and a GRU--CNN--BiLSTM--LSTM ensemble \cite{huynh2020ensemble} attains 65.79\% macro F1. Despite these advances, minority emotions such as Fear,
Anger, and Surprise remain difficult to classify and leave room for data-centric and
ensemble improvements.

\subsection{LLM-based Augmentation and Ensemble Methods}

Ubani et al.\cite{ubani2023zeroshotdataaug} show zero-shot ChatGPT generation outperforms EDA
and back-translation on SST-2 and SNIPS; Whitehouse et al.\
\cite{whitehouse2023llmaug} extend LLM augmentation to cross-lingual settings; \"{U}veges and Ring
\cite{uveges2025synthetic} confirm 10-shot LLM prompting yields macro-F1 gains for
underrepresented emotion classes. However, prior work treats the LLM as an offline
generator only, or as a static zero-/few-shot classifier, and prompt design for
fine-grained Vietnamese emotion categories remains underexplored. On the ensemble
side, Thin et al.\ \cite{thin2024ensemble}
obtain 94.03\% weighted F1 on VSFC \cite{nguyen2018vsfc} via soft-voting over five Vietnamese transformers;
Thin et al.\ \cite{thin2024prompt} further evaluate zero- and few-shot LLM prompting for Vietnamese sentiment. This
leaves a methodological gap for Vietnamese emotion recognition: using an LLM both
to generate minority-class data and to combine heterogeneous ensemble probabilities
while conditioning on the sentence. We address that gap with the two-stage
framework in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}.

We address this by prompting Gemini-2.5-Flash \cite{comanici2025gemini} via LangChain \cite{Chase_LangChain_2022} to add training examples for the underrepresented emotions, on the raw training split before any preprocessing. Table~\ref{tab:gen_examples} shows examples of both. For each class below 1,500 examples, we generated enough candidates to reach a pre-deduplication target of 1,500; deduplication then left several final counts slightly below that threshold (Table~\ref{tab:class-counts}).

\subsection{Text Preprocessing}

Vietnamese social-media text is noisy: users mix emojis, keyboard emoticons, slang, and
short forms that carry strong emotional meaning. If these signals are removed too early,
models may lose useful cues. Following practices similar to prior Vietnamese social-media
emotion work \cite{nguyen2020exploiting,duong2021review}, we therefore apply a shared
cleaning pipeline to the data after augmentation.

Each sentence is cleaned in four steps. First, a curated dictionary of emoji-to-Vietnamese mappings replaces Unicode emojis with short descriptive phrases (e.g., ``vui vẻ'' for
happy faces and ``buồn bã'' for sad faces); unmatched emojis are then removed with a
Unicode regex filter. Second, text emoticons such as \texttt{:))}, \texttt{:(}, and
\texttt{T\_T} are mapped to Vietnamese words, with longer patterns matched first to avoid partial replacements. Third, common internet abbreviations are expanded at the word level (e.g., \textit{ko}/\textit{k}/\textit{hok}, all meaning \textit{không};
\textit{dc}/\textit{đc}, meaning \textit{được}). Fourth, URLs and HTML tags are stripped, non-Vietnamese characters are replaced with spaces while diacritics and basic punctuation are kept, repeated punctuation is collapsed, and whitespace is normalized.

After cleaning, duplicate sentences are removed so repeated content does not bias training. Stopwords are intentionally kept. Function words such as \textit{không} (negation) and \textit{rất} (intensifier) often change emotion meaning, and BERT-style encoders can use
these tokens through self-attention \cite{nguyen2020phobert,do2024vlue}.

\subsection{Base Classifiers}
Five heterogeneous base classifiers—PhoBERT-v2 \cite{nguyen2020phobert}, CafeBERT \cite{do2024vlue}, ViBERT \cite{the2020improving}, Logistic Regression \cite{lin2023linear}, and LinearSVC—are trained with stratified 3-fold cross-validation, each producing probability vectors across the seven emotion classes.

Each model is also refitted on the full training set to produce validation and test
probability arrays.

\subsection{Stacked Ensemble with Meta-learning LLM}

\textbf{Weights and structured dataset.}
Each base model receives one weight, computed on the validation split after that model has been refit on the entire training set. For model~$i$, $\mathrm{Acc}_i$ is the accuracy of the model on the validation data. The five accuracies are then scaled so the weights sum to one:
\begin{equation}
  W_i = \frac{\mathrm{Acc}_i}{\sum_{j=1}^{M} \mathrm{Acc}_j}, \qquad M = 5.
  \label{eq:ensemble_weight}
\end{equation}
A model that classifies more of the validation split correctly therefore takes a larger share of the weighted-average distribution. The five weights are calculated once, before LLM meta-learning training, and the same values are reused in the training, validation, and test prompts.

During meta-learning model training, each training sentence is paired with the probabilities distribution from the models, and $W_i$ is applied to those out-of-fold distributions to form the weighted average. Validation and test prompts use the distributions from the full-training refit, because those sentences were held out of base-model fitting. Every prompt contains the original sentence, the five per-model distributions, and that weighted average, so the meta-learning model reads the text together with the ensemble signals.


\textbf{QLoRA fine-tuning.}
Gemma-2-9B-it \cite{gemma_2024} serves as the predictor. The model is
fine-tuned with QLoRA \cite{dettmers2023qlora} using completion-only on the emotion label as the target output. At inference, the
model generates the emotion label jointly conditioned on the
sentence and the five base-model probability distributions in
the structured prompt.

No generated sentence matches the test set exactly or nearly, so the gain in Table~\ref{tab:augmentation} is not copied evaluation text. Fidelity is 0.776, the average of four scores between 0 and 1. Statistical similarity compares the average and the spread of sentence length, in characters and in words. Distribution similarity uses the Kolmogorov--Smirnov statistic on those length distributions. Label similarity is \(\exp(-D_{\mathrm{KL}})\) on the mix of emotions. \(D_{\mathrm{KL}} = 0.164\) bits is the shift from filling every class below 1,500 examples, and that term equals 0.849. Correlation similarity is 0.850. It checks whether length and emotion still move together: \(r\) is the Pearson correlation of the two upper triangles, and \(F\) is the normalized Frobenius distance between the correlation matrices. Average length stays close, 56 characters against 51, but generated sentences vary much less, and the Kolmogorov--Smirnov tests reject equal length and word-count distributions (\(p \approx 0\)). Distinct-2 drops from 0.593 to 0.451 and Distinct-3 from 0.930 to 0.786, because we were trying to create more sentences, which used the same Vietnamese vocabulary. The new sentences are a little more uniform than the original posts, and the emotion mix is shifted because the scarce classes were filled in.

\subsection{Ensemble Performance}
The stacked QLoRA model is the strongest system on our split.
Table~\ref{tab:ensemble} reports 70.33\% $\pm$ 1.33 accuracy, 70.39\% $\pm$ 1.26 weighted F1, and 67.27\% $\pm$ 1.25 macro F1, averaged over seeds 42, 131, and 9998.

Weighted soft voting and logistic-regression stacking reaches 67.91\% weighted F1.
Logistic regression reaches 67.48\%. QLoRA improves on these by 2.48 and 2.91 weighted-F1 percentage points. The macro-F1 gap against logistic regression is wider: 67.27\% against 63.27\%, a gain of 4.00 percentage points.
Both baselines already combine the five base models, so the remaining gain comes from reading the sentence together with their probabilities.

The zero shot prompting score 65.72\% weighted F1, 4.67 percentage points behind the fine-tuned model. A single transformer trained on the same files is further behind: CafeBERT reaches 65.10\% $\pm$ 0.40 weighted F1 and ViSoBERT 58.05\% $\pm$ 0.54, leaving the full ensemble ahead by 5.29 and 12.34 percentage points.
Published scores in the lower block of Table~\ref{tab:ensemble} come from other preprocessing and evaluation settings, intended to provide additional reference information regarding existing, similar methods..

\section{Conclusion}

\subsection{Summary}

This paper proposed a two-stage framework for Vietnamese social-media emotion
recognition. Stage~1 shows that generated few-shot augmentation improves CafeBERT
on the underrepresented emotions more than paraphrasing
(Table~\ref{tab:augmentation}). And data augmentation with LLM benefits the performace of models. Stage~2 shows that the QLoRA Gemma meta-learning model,
trained on the sentence and the base-model probabilities, outperforms the
same-protocol comparators (Table~\ref{tab:ensemble}).
\subsection{Limitations \& Future Work}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Research limitations:}
    This pipeline trained three different BERT models using three folds to extract probabilities for the final joint data, which requires significant GPU computation. Moreover, fine-tuning the Gemma model also needs the same computational power. All of the experiments were conducted on an NVIDIA A100 80GB GPU.
  \item \textbf{Cross-dataset and cross-domain generalization:}
        Future work should evaluate the framework on related Vietnamese NLP tasks
        (aspect-level sentiment, hate-speech detection, multi-label corpora) to verify
        that LLM augmentation and stacked ensembling generalize beyond fine-grained
        emotion recognition.
\end{enumerate}
